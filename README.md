# Granular Data, Hidden Risks: The Ethics of Smart Meter IoT Data Collection by UK Energy Firms

Coursework for COM7023 Responsible Computing, part of my MSc in Artificial Intelligence and Data Science at Leeds Trinity University.

This is my Assessment 2 investigative report. It runs to eight pages including references, and the PDF is the only file in this repo.

Its starting point is that half-hourly meter readings reveal more than a bill needs: when someone gets up, whether they were home overnight, which appliances they run. The report asks whether the benefits of the UK rollout (accurate bills, a more efficient grid, lower carbon) justify that exposure.

## What the report argues

The technology is useful, but consent is weaker than it looks and the rules around it pull in different directions.

- **Consent in practice.** Opt-in requirements exist, but the explanations are hard to follow, so agreeing to a meter and understanding what happens to the data are two different things.
- **Regulators disagree.** In December 2025 the ICO objected to Ofgem's plan for half-hourly settlement for all consumers, and no higher authority settles that kind of clash.
- **Harm falls unevenly.** Elderly, low-income and digitally excluded households carry most of the risk, while the financial gains mostly go to energy firms and technology companies.
- **Developers have duties too.** The BCS and ACM codes put privacy obligations on the people who build these systems, whatever their employer wants.

I analysed this through three lenses: Nissenbaum's contextual integrity, Kant on consent, and a Mill-style weighing of costs and benefits. The report ends with five recommendations: plain-English consent, resolving the ICO and Ofgem conflict, specific protections for vulnerable households, impact assessments and data minimisation at design time, and audit powers over third parties.
